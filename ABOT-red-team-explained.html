How each entry is laid out. Every point gets a plain-English title and a badge line, then three parts:

  • What it is: what the paper says, and what a critic says about it.
  • Why it's an issue: why that matters, in everyday terms.
  • Impact on your talk: what to do on stage.

When a point bundles several problems, What it is and Why it's an issue number them the same way, so problem 1 matches reason 1.

Reading the badge line. Example:

CriticalHard to defend75/100Paper: V-C

  • Priority is how much damage the point does if it holds:
    • Critical: it undermines a headline claim.
    • Major: it undermines a secondary claim or the paper's framing.
    • Minor: presentation, citations or wording.
  • Hard to defend (1–100) is how hard the paper's position is to justify once someone raises the point. 1 means a convincing answer exists; 100 means all you can do is concede. It is not how likely anyone is to raise it. A typo can't be defended, so it scores high, but nobody will ask about it.
  • Paper gives the paper section the point is about.
  • !Outside fact marks a point that relies on an outside fact nobody has verified yet. Check it before you quote it.

Two cautions.

  • The suggested replies in the entries come from the red team and the triage. Where one says something is "next", "planned" or "future work", say it only if it matches your actual plans.
  • A few entries mention what the red team found when it checked the code. Those notes only explain why a paper-level point holds. They aren't extra points to raise.

Part 1 of 3 · 10 points · 6 questions

Have an answer ready

These 10 points could realistically come up in Q&A, from anyone who has read the paper or knows mesh networking. They boil down to six questions. Each question starts with a short, honest answer to learn. The entries under it explain the points behind it.

Question 1

"Everyone reaches a safe zone ~100% of the time. Is the task too easy?"

Your answer

"Yes. Each node has about 30 neighbours, so most routes are only 2–3 hops. In this version a hop succeeds as long as the next node is still working, which is why we compare methods on route length. Next we'll make the task harder: sparser meshes, and hops that can fail on weak links."

Points behind this question: P-05.

P-05Answer ready · Q1

Every method succeeds, so the test can't show robustness

CriticalHard to defend80/100Paper: V-A; V-B

What it is

The paper says every method reaches a safe zone (the rescue point a route must reach) at its ceiling (its highest possible success rate), so the methods separate on path efficiency (how short their routes are). A critic raises three problems:

The problemWhy it's an issue
  1. 1
    Problem 1: Every method succeeds about 100% of the time, at every damage level (the five presets, intact to extreme). The methods are four planners (classical route-finding methods) and the PPO agent (a reinforcement-learning route chooser, trained by trial and error).
    Why it's an issueWhen every method succeeds, the test can't show which one copes better with damage. This is built into the simulation: the red team checked the code, and a hop always succeeds as long as the next node is still working, however weak the radio link.
  2. 2
    Problem 2: Only one planner's score responds materially to damage, and only as a single step, between heavy and severe. Every other method moves by less than 0.02.
    Why it's an issueIf damage barely changes the results, the damage levels aren't really being tested: apart from that one step, the results look almost the same from intact to extreme.
  3. 3
    Problem 3: Routes are short: the paper's median routes are 2–4 hops (radio jumps) long, on a mesh where each node (one radio device) has about 30 neighbours on average (30.7).
    Why it's an issueShort routes on a dense mesh leave little room for one method to beat another, so the task is too easy to show robustness.

The task can't tell a robust method from a fragile one, so a reader may doubt that routing under damage was really tested.

Impact on your talk

Have an answer ready (Question 1). Don't present the ~100% success as evidence of robustness to damage. Say that methods are compared on route length, because success is at its ceiling (its highest possible level).

Question 2

"How many SOS messages actually reach a rescuer, and how fast?"

Your answer

"This paper doesn't measure that yet; it compares the routes each method picks. End-to-end SOS delivery and delay are the next experiment."

Points behind this question: P-01.

P-01Answer ready · Q2

The paper never measures whether an SOS reaches a rescuer

CriticalHard to defend85/100Paper: Abstract; V; VII

What it is

The paper says a survivor's distress message (an SOS) travels hop by hop (one radio jump at a time) to an active rescue point, and that its results show "improved packet delivery performance". A critic raises three problems:

The problemWhy it's an issue
  1. 1
    Problem 1: No end-to-end delivery ratio (the share of SOS messages that reach a rescuer, from start to finish): the paper never reports how often an SOS reaches a rescue point.
    Why it's an issueFor a disaster system, the core question is whether a distress message reaches help at all, and the paper cannot yet answer it.
  2. 2
    Problem 2: No delay (latency, how long a message takes to arrive): the paper never reports how long an SOS takes to arrive.
    Why it's an issueIn an emergency, how long a message takes matters too, and the paper gives no timing for SOS arrival.
  3. 3
    Problem 3: Its "mean link PDR" (PDR, packet delivery ratio, normally means the share of messages that get through) is never defined, and nothing it reports is delivery to a rescuer. Its conclusion still refers to "the measured delivery gain".
    Why it's an issueThe delivery wording rests on figures that don't measure delivery to a rescuer, so a critic can say the delivery claim is untested.

Overall, the system's central promise, getting an SOS to rescuers, isn't measured yet.

Impact on your talk

Have an answer ready (Question 2). This is the most natural question for a disaster-response audience. Never claim "improved packet delivery" or "the delivery gain" on a slide. Consider an optional "Next steps" slide that lists end-to-end SOS delivery and delay, so the answer is already on screen when someone asks.

P-03Answer ready · Q3

The planner needs a full map the protocol never builds

CriticalHard to defend85/100Paper: III (routing layer); V

What it is

The paper describes a fully decentralised design, with no central coordinator, whose routing uses live network state for dynamic Dijkstra (a classic route-finding method, a kind of planner). A critic raises four problems:

The problemWhy it's an issue
  1. 1
    Problem 1: Beacons (short radio messages used to discover neighbours) carry only the sender's ID, so they can't spread link-state information.
    Why it's an issueWithout a way to spread link state, a node can't build the whole-network picture that global Dijkstra needs, so the protocol can't run it.
  2. 2
    Problem 2: A route-update message type (ROUTE_UPDATE) is listed but never described.
    Why it's an issueA message type that is listed but never described gives the protocol no defined way to share routing updates.
  3. 3
    Problem 3: The evaluated planners are handed a full, up-to-date snapshot of the network (a "global snapshot") by the simulation.
    Why it's an issueBecause the simulator hands the planners the whole network, the results show what a planner could do with a complete picture, not what nodes can do with this protocol.
  4. 4
    Problem 4: The radio time (airtime, the time a radio spends sending) that spreading link state would cost on a slow SF12 channel (a long-range setting of LoRa, a low-power radio) is not modelled.
    Why it's an issueSharing link state would use radio time, and the results leave that cost out.

The supported result holds only for an all-knowing ("oracle") planner, not for the decentralised protocol described.

Impact on your talk

Have an answer ready (Question 3). Don't imply the evaluated planner runs on the decentralised protocol as described. On slides, describe the planners as having a full view of the network, as a best case.

Question 4

"A 'link PDR' of 0.27, yet every route succeeds? Isn't the planner just maximising its own score?"

Your answer

"The number is a link-quality score for the nodes along the route, not the share of messages delivered. And partly yes: the dynamic planner uses that same score to choose its routes. So we only claim that live link state changes which routes get picked, with about 1.8× higher scores. We don't claim yet that it improves delivery."

What "for the nodes along the route" means: the red team checked the code, and the score averages all the links of each node the route visits, not just the links the route actually used.

Points behind this question: P-04, P-07, P-22.

P-04Answer ready · Q4

The link scores can't be per-hop delivery chances

CriticalHard to defend82/100Paper: V-A; V-C

What it is

The paper reports about 100% success for every method, alongside a "mean link PDR" of 0.145 for static Dijkstra and 0.267 for dynamic Dijkstra. (PDR, packet delivery ratio, normally means the share of messages that get through.) A critic raises three problems:

The problemWhy it's an issue
  1. 1
    Problem 1: Read as per-hop delivery chances (the chance that each hop, or single radio jump, works), 0.145 and 0.267 give end-to-end survival of about 1.5% vs 1.4% (0.145 to the power of 2.17 vs 0.267 to the power of 3.23, using average route lengths of 2.17 and 3.23 hops): a tie.
    Why it's an issueOn that reading the two planners tie, so the difference the paper reports between them would disappear.
  2. 2
    Problem 2: Both are absurd next to ~100% success, so the numbers can't mean what the paper implies.
    Why it's an issueBoth figures clash with the near-100% success those planners achieve, so a reader can't take them as delivery chances.
  3. 3
    Problem 3: Averaging per-hop values rewards longer paths and hides how losses compound hop after hop.
    Why it's an issueReal losses multiply hop after hop, so longer routes should look worse. An average of per-hop scores ignores this, so it can't show how many messages actually get through.

The red team calls this only an illustration, since the number isn't a per-hop chance, but a reviewer who takes the paper at its word will do this arithmetic.

Impact on your talk

Have an answer ready (Question 4). Don't call 0.267 or 0.145 "delivery" or "the PDR of the route". Call them link-quality scores.

P-07Answer ready · Q4

The winning planner is graded on the score it chases

CriticalHard to defend75/100Paper: V-C

What it is

The paper's main supported result is that taking live link quality out of the dynamic Dijkstra planner lowers the "mean link PDR" along its routes (a link-quality score; PDR normally means the share of messages that get through) from 0.267 to 0.145. But that planner chooses its routes using PDR, and is then scored on PDR. A critic will say: remove the thing a planner aims for, and of course it scores lower on that thing.

Why it's an issue

It's like coaching a student on one practice test and then grading them on that same test: a higher mark shows the coaching worked, not that they learned more. The result shows that live link quality changes which routes get picked. It doesn't show that more messages get through, which is what the paper's "improved packet delivery" suggests.

Impact on your talk

Have an answer ready (Question 4). On slides, describe the result narrowly: live link-quality scores change the routes, giving about 1.8× higher scores. Never say "better delivery" or "the delivery gain".

P-22Answer ready · Q4

The paper uses PDR for three different quantities

MajorHard to defend65/100Paper: III; IV; V

What it is

The paper uses "PDR" (packet delivery ratio, normally the share of messages that get through) throughout. A critic points out that the term covers at least three different quantities:

The problemWhy it's an issue
  1. 1
    Problem 1: Fig. 2's "PDR" is the channel model's prediction at a given distance (the channel model is the formulas for how radio signals weaken with distance).
    Why it's an issueA prediction for a radio link at a given distance is not a measurement of messages reaching rescuers, so Fig. 2 cannot support a delivery claim.
  2. 2
    Problem 2: The results' "mean link PDR" (V-B and V-C) is never defined.
    Why it's an issueA key measure is left undefined, so a reader can't check what the number means.
  3. 3
    Problem 3: The PDR compared in the KS tests (a statistical check that compares the two simulators) is a third, different quantity. The red team checked the code: it counts a message as delivered as soon as any one other node (radio device) hears it.
    Why it's an issueThe KS-test PDR counts a message as delivered as soon as one other node hears it, so it can't stand in for delivery to a rescuer.

None of the three is SOS delivery, and a reader can't tell which PDR a number means.

Impact on your talk

Have an answer ready (Question 4). On slides, label each number precisely, for example "link-quality score along the route" or "PDR vs distance from the channel model". Never use a bare "PDR" for two different things.

Question 5

"How does this compare with Meshtastic, or with mesh routing such as OLSR/ETX? What's new over [5]?"

P-10Answer ready · Q5

None of the baselines is a decentralised protocol

MajorHard to defend78/100Paper: V

What it is

The paper pitches a decentralised mesh (devices relaying messages for each other) and compares it with four baselines (reference methods): static and dynamic Dijkstra and A*, which are classical route-finding methods (planners). A critic raises three problems:

The problemWhy it's an issue
  1. 1
    Problem 1: None of the four baselines is a decentralised protocol (one where each node decides using only what it can hear). Missing from the comparison are plain flooding (every node repeats every message), ABOT's own gossip forwarding used alone (re-sending with a set probability), Meshtastic's managed flooding (Meshtastic is an open-source mesh platform), on-demand protocols such as AODV or DSR (which find routes when needed), link-quality protocols such as OLSR, B.A.T.M.A.N. or Babel, and greedy geographic routing (GPSR, which forwards each message by position).
    Why it's an issueWithout decentralised protocols, the paper cannot show how its design stacks up against methods a real mesh could use.
  2. 2
    Problem 2: All four baselines are handed a full snapshot of the network (a complete picture at one moment).
    Why it's an issueThe planners see more than any real node could, so their results show a best case (an upper bound), not what a real node can achieve.
  3. 3
    Problem 3: Greedy geographic forwarding is part of ABOT's own design, yet it is not used as a baseline.
    Why it's an issueLeaving it out means the paper never tests one of its own forwarding rules against the other methods.

Overall, a paper about decentralisation compares itself only with centralised methods, so it has not yet shown how it stands against other decentralised designs.

Impact on your talk

Have an answer ready (Question 5). Don't claim that ABOT beats existing mesh protocols, because none was tested. Call the baselines "classical planners with a full view of the network", and say they are upper bounds (best-case comparisons), with protocol baselines still planned.

P-11Answer ready · Q5

Live link-quality routing already exists in mesh protocols

MajorHard to defend75/100Paper: II (gap 2)!Outside fact

What it is

The paper's second gap says that existing link-quality-aware routing acts on a static topology (a fixed network layout) or recomputes only after a failure. A critic raises three problems:

The problemWhy it's an issue
  1. 1
    Problem 1: A per-link score of 1 divided by PDR (the share of messages that get through) is ETX, a classic measure from 2003 (expected transmission count: roughly how many tries a message needs to cross a link).
    Why it's an issueIf the score is ETX, the measure is old, so the paper's claim to novelty has to rest on how it applies that measure.
  2. 2
    Problem 2: OLSR-ETX, B.A.T.M.A.N. and Babel (mesh-routing protocols) already update link quality continuously from hello statistics (measurements from routine "hello" messages between neighbours), which contradicts the paper's stated gap.
    Why it's an issueThe paper presents this gap as one of three reasons ABOT is needed. If existing protocols already do this, that reason falls away.
  3. 3
    Problem 3: None of this work is cited.
    Why it's an issueLeaving out well-known work makes it look as if the authors missed it, and a networking audience will notice.

Taken together, the stated gap, and so the paper's case for novelty, is easy to challenge.

Impact on your talk

Have an answer ready (Question 5). Don't say "first" or "a novel routing metric". Frame the novelty as applying live link quality to a gateway-free LoRa mesh (a long-range, low-power radio network with no fixed gateway station) under five damage levels, not as the metric itself.

!Relies on an outside fact; check it before quoting it.

P-06Answer ready · Q6

Beacons alone would saturate the radio channel at this density

CriticalHard to defend80/100Paper: III; VI

What it is

The paper's setup uses SF12 (the slowest, longest-range setting of LoRa, a low-power radio, so each message takes a long time to send) and beacons every 30 s (short messages neighbours use to measure links), its mesh averages 30.7 neighbours per node, and it says the beacons take "roughly 4.4% channel occupancy". That works out to each node's own airtime (the time its radio is busy sending; see P-20). A critic raises three problems:

The problemWhy it's an issue
  1. 1
    Problem 1: Beacon load: each receiver hears about 30.7 neighbours × 4.4% = about 1.35 Erlang (a unit of channel load; 1 means always busy) of beacon traffic alone, or about 2.0 with the 34-byte header (the fixed front part of each message).
    Why it's an issueAt 1.35 Erlang, the beacons alone would need more than all of the airtime around each receiver: more traffic than the channel can carry.
  2. 2
    Problem 2: Under the simple ALOHA model (senders transmit whenever they like, and overlapping messages collide), only about 2–7% of transmissions would get through.
    Why it's an issueIf only 2–7% of transmissions survive, most beacons are lost, and any SOS message has to compete for the same crowded channel.
  3. 3
    Problem 3: So the channel saturates before any SOS (distress) traffic is sent, and beacon-based link monitoring can't work at this density.
    Why it's an issueRouting depends on that live link picture, so if beacon-based monitoring cannot work at this density, routing would lack reliable link information.

Taken together, the sum uses only the paper's own numbers, so the objection is hard to dismiss.

Impact on your talk

Have an answer ready (Question 6). Don't present beacon-based link monitoring as proven to work at this density. The paper lists duty cycle (the share of time a device may transmit) as a limitation in Section VI, so the honest line is that it's a known limit. If asked what to do, say that beacon rate or spreading factor would have to adapt at this density, and that adaptive beaconing (changing how often beacons are sent) is the planned study.

P-02Watch your wording

Episodes last seconds, not a 33-minute window

CriticalHard to defend85/100Paper: III; RL env; V-A

What it is

The paper calls its 200-step episode cap, about 33 minutes, "a representative post-disaster routing window", and a critic raises five problems (an episode is one simulated run; a step is 10 simulated seconds):

The problemWhy it's an issue
  1. 1
    Problem 1: Typical (median, the middle value) episodes last 2–4 steps, or 20–40 simulated seconds, not 33 minutes.
    Why it's an issueA reader would picture a 33-minute window, but the results describe a run of seconds.
  2. 2
    Problem 2: In that time there is at most one beacon round: an "I'm here" message from every node, every 30 seconds.
    Why it's an issueThe link-quality measures come from beacons, and at most one round fits, so there is little to measure.
  3. 3
    Problem 3: The window used to measure link quality, 50 packets (messages) or about 25 minutes, can't fill.
    Why it's an issueThe link-quality window never completes, so the measure rests on a partly filled window.
  4. 4
    Problem 4: A given node (one radio device) has only about a 0.8% chance of failing in any 30 seconds. That comes to about 0.4 sudden failures per 100-node episode, since half of failures are gradual (a slow battery drain needing 300 seconds).
    Why it's an issueFailures are too rare to test the failure handling: there is almost nothing for failures to do.
  5. 5
    Problem 5: The battery never drains to the 20% threshold, below which a node's deliveries are weakened.
    Why it's an issueThe battery never reaches the level that changes a delivery, so that model has no effect here.

Overall, the disaster dynamics that motivate the paper barely act.

Impact on your talk

Watch your wording. Leave the 33-minute figure out, and say that episodes last a few steps. If asked about failures or battery drain, use the backup answer below.

P-19Set aside

The replanning explanation contradicts itself

MajorHard to defend70/100Paper: V-C, para. 3

What it is

The paper says both dynamic planners (route-finding methods) start from an empty network snapshot (every link weighted equally), so any separation from their static counterparts comes from replanning (recalculating a route) alone. The same paragraph then restricts the analysis to the 43 of 100 seeds (fixed random scenarios) where first routes match, and a critic reads this as conceding that in the other 57 the routes differ because of how ties between equally good routes are broken.

Why it's an issue

The paragraph contradicts itself, which undermines the claim it is trying to make: it gives replanning as the sole cause, then, on the critic's reading, concedes another cause in 57 of the 100 seeds.

Impact on your talk

Safe to set aside. Don't say "any separation comes from replanning alone." If asked, the red team's suggested answer is that the full-sample ratio includes tie-breaking effects, and the 43-seed subset isolates replanning.

(Two of the three reviewers ticked "best paper" on the version they saw.)
  • The infrastructure is real. The two simulators, the RL training environment, the statistics pipeline and an audit trail that re-derives the numbers all exist.
  • A narrow claim holds. In simulation, a planner that re-plans on a live link-quality score picks routes through nodes that score higher on it. That shows the pipeline works end to end. It doesn't yet show better delivery.

Glossary

Plain-English meanings of the technical terms used in this guide, in alphabetical order.

A*
A faster version of Dijkstra's shortest-path method; it uses a distance estimate (a "heuristic") to search in the right direction first.
Ablation
An experiment that switches one part off to see whether it matters.
Action masking
Hiding invalid choices (such as dead neighbours) so the agent can't pick them.
Action space
The set of choices the agent has at each step. In ABOT there are 8 slots, each pointing to a neighbour in a fixed list sorted by ID number.
Agent / policy
The trained decision-maker (here, PPO). The policy is its rule for turning what it sees into a choice.
Airtime / duty cycle
The share of time a radio spends transmitting. Some regions cap it by law; Europe's ETSI rule sets 1% for this kind of band.
ALOHA / slotted ALOHA
The simplest way to share a radio channel: send whenever you have something, and overlapping messages collide. In slotted ALOHA, senders start only at the beginning of shared time slots, which needs synchronised clocks.
AODV / DSR
Routing protocols for ad-hoc networks that look for a route only when a message needs one.
At ceiling
When a score can't go any higher (here, success near 100%), so it can no longer tell methods apart.
Baseline
A standard method you compare your own method against.
Beacon
A short "I'm here" message that every ABOT node broadcasts every 30 seconds, so neighbours can judge how good the link is.
Capture effect
When two messages overlap, the receiver can still read the much stronger one; in the paper, it must be at least 6 dB stronger than the other.
Confidence interval / bootstrap
A range showing how precise a number is. The bootstrap estimates this by recomputing the number on many randomly re-drawn copies of the data.
Contention
Nodes competing for the same radio channel, so messages sent at the same time can collide.
Critic / explained variance
PPO's critic is the part that predicts future reward. Explained variance scores those predictions: 1 is perfect; 0 or below is no better than always guessing the average.
Damage levels (presets)
The five settings in the paper (intact, moderate, heavy, severe, extreme) that make radio signals fade faster with distance, to mimic more destruction. "Heavy" is the default.
dB (decibel)
A unit for signal strength or loss, on a scale where every 10 dB is a factor of ten. More loss in dB means a weaker signal.
Decentralised
No central controller; every node decides for itself using what it can hear.
Dijkstra (static / dynamic)
The classic method for finding the cheapest route through a network. Static Dijkstra counts hops; dynamic Dijkstra weights links by live link quality and re-plans when a link ahead dies or weakens.
Episode
One simulated run, from a start node until it reaches a safe zone or time runs out.
Erlang
A unit of channel load. 1 Erlang is enough traffic to keep a channel busy all the time; above 1, there is more traffic than the channel can carry.
ETSI EN 300 220-2
A European standard for short-range radio devices, including duty-cycle limits. It doesn't govern India's 865–867 MHz band.
ETX (expected transmission count)
A classic link-quality measure from 2003: roughly how many tries a message needs to cross a link, about 1 divided by the link's PDR (the share of messages that get through).
Fast simulator / reference simulator
The paper's two simulators. The fast (timestep) simulator produced the results; the slower reference (SimPy) simulator was used to check it.
Flooding
Every node rebroadcasts every new message it hears.
Gateway
In a standard LoRa network (LoRaWAN), the base station that collects messages from devices and connects them to the internet. ABOT is designed to work without one.
Gossip (Gossip3)
Forwarding where each node rebroadcasts a message with some probability (0.75 in ABOT), and stops rebroadcasting once it has heard 3 copies.
Greedy geographic routing (GPSR)
Passing each message to the neighbour closest to its destination, using the nodes' positions.
Half-duplex
A radio that can't send and receive at the same time.
Hash seed
A hidden setting of the programming language (Python) that, in ABOT's code, decides how ties between equally good routes are broken. Changing it shifts one planner's scores slightly.
Header / MTU
The header is the addressing and control part of a message (34 bytes in the paper); the MTU is the largest message allowed (51 bytes in the paper).
Holm correction
A way of adjusting p-values when several statistical tests are run at once, so that chance findings don't slip through.
Hop
One radio jump from a node to a neighbour.
Jitter
A small random delay added to when a message is sent, so that nodes don't all transmit at the same moment.
KS test (Kolmogorov–Smirnov)
A statistical test of whether two sets of numbers look as if they come from the same source. The paper uses it to compare its two simulators.
Listen-before-talk (CAD)
Checking that the channel is free before sending. LoRa radios can do this with Channel Activity Detection (CAD).
Livelock
The agent keeps repeating a useless action and never makes progress, even though nothing has crashed.
LoRa / LoRaWAN
LoRa is a long-range, low-power radio technology. LoRaWAN is the standard network system built on it, which relies on gateways; DR0 is its slowest data-rate setting.
Mann–Whitney U / Wilcoxon signed-rank
Tests of whether one group's values tend to be larger than another's. Mann–Whitney is for unrelated groups; Wilcoxon signed-rank (or sign test) is the paired version, used when both methods ran the same scenarios.
Median / IQR
The median is the middle value. The IQR (inter-quartile range) is the span covered by the middle half of the values.
Mesh network
A network where every device also relays messages for others, so no single device is essential.
Meshtastic
Popular open-source firmware (a device's built-in software) that turns off-the-shelf LoRa boards (such as T-Beam, Heltec or RAK) into a text-messaging mesh.
Node
One radio device in the mesh.
ns-3 / ns3-gym
ns-3 is a widely used network simulator; ns3-gym connects it to the standard interface used for reinforcement learning.
Observation
The 24 numbers PPO sees at each step, such as its position, battery level and the link quality to its neighbours.
OLSR, B.A.T.M.A.N., Babel
Well-known routing protocols (sets of rules for passing messages on) for mesh networks; they keep measuring link quality from regular hello messages.
Oracle / global snapshot
A full, up-to-date map of the whole network that the simulation hands to a planner. A real decentralised node wouldn't have it.
Path loss / shadowing
Path loss is how much a radio signal weakens over distance; the path-loss exponent controls how fast this happens. Shadowing (σ) adds random variation caused by obstacles.
PDR (packet delivery ratio)
The share of messages that get through. The paper uses the term for several different quantities (see P-22).
Pearson r
A number from −1 to +1 showing how closely two quantities move together.
Post hoc subgroup
A subset of the data chosen after looking at the results. Findings from it are weaker evidence.
PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization)
A standard training method for reinforcement learning, used here to train the route-choosing agent.
p-value
Roughly, how likely a difference this big would be if there were no real difference. Below 0.05 is the usual cut-off for "statistically significant".
Radio horizon
The distance beyond which the Earth's curvature blocks the straight line between two antennas; roughly 8–10 km for antennas 1–2 m above the ground.
Rank-biserial correlation (r_rb)
An effect size from −1 to +1 showing how strongly one group tends to beat the other. In the paper's Table II, negative values favour PPO.
Reinforcement learning (RL)
Training an agent by trial and error, rewarding good outcomes.
Reward
The score the agent receives during training. In ABOT: +100 for reaching a safe zone, −1 per step, and penalties for bad choices.
RSSI
Received signal strength, that is, how strong a signal is when it arrives.
Safe zone
The paper's word for the rescue point a route must reach; in the simulation, two fixed corner nodes.
Seed
A number that fixes all the random choices, so a scenario can be replayed exactly. A "training seed" is one independent training run.
Sequence number
A counter carried in each message, so a receiver can tell how many it missed.
Spreading factor (SF12)
A LoRa setting that trades speed for range. SF12 is the slowest and longest-range; even a short message takes more than a second to send.
UWB (ultra-wideband)
A short-range radio technology, very different from LoRa.
Value estimate, V(s)
PPO's prediction of how much future reward a situation is worth. ABOT's gossip shares these between nodes, and the paper calls them a "Q-table".
