Day 1 · about 1 h · Foundation↑ map

Networks 101

A network is a set of devices (nodes) that pass messages (packets) to each other over links. ABOT is a mesh with no central base station: every device sends its own messages and relays other people's.

Concepts

NodeCore
One device on the network. In ABOT, a LoRa radio carried by a survivor or fixed in place as a relay.
LinkCore
A connection between two nodes that can hear each other.
Packet, header, payloadCore
A packet is one message. The header carries addressing and control fields; the payload is the content.
Multi-hopCore
A message crosses several links, relayed node to node, to reach a destination outside direct range.
Broadcast vs unicastCore
Broadcast goes to everyone in range. Unicast is addressed to one chosen neighbour.
Star vs mesh topologyCore
In a star, such as LoRaWAN, every device talks to a central gateway. In a mesh, devices talk to each other and relay.
Single point of failureCore
A component whose loss takes down the whole system. A gateway is one.
Protocol stackCore
The network's job split into layers, each relying on the one below. ABOT's Fig. 1 has four: Physical (LoRa), MAC and Forwarding, Link-State Monitoring, Routing and Evacuation.
Control vs data trafficDeeper
Beacons and route updates keep the network running; SOS alerts are what it exists for. Control traffic costs airtime.
Ad hoc network (MANET)Deeper
A network that forms itself without infrastructure. ABOT belongs to this family.

In ABOT

  • 0 gatewaysEvery node is both terminal and relay. Fully peer-to-peer.
  • below 50%Delivery can drop under half when many devices share few gateways (reference [1]). This is the bottleneck ABOT removes.
  • 5 typesBEACON, SOS_ALERT, ROUTE_UPDATE, ACK, GOSSIP.
  • 4 layersFig. 1. The text says "three functional layers"; the figure splits MAC and forwarding into its own layer.

Radio physics

Range comes down to three quantities: how much power leaves the transmitter, how much is lost on the way, and how faint a signal the receiver can still pick out of background noise. Engineers track all three in decibels so they can simply add and subtract.

Concepts

Frequency and bandCore
How fast the radio wave oscillates, in MHz. A band is a range of frequencies set aside for a use.
ISM bandCore
Frequencies anyone may use without a licence, within power and airtime rules. In India, 865–867 MHz.
Decibel (dB)Core
A ratio on a log scale. +3 dB ≈ 2× the power, +6 dB ≈ 4×, +10 dB = 10×. Losses and gains add instead of multiplying.
dBmCore
Absolute power in dB relative to 1 milliwatt. 0 dBm = 1 mW; +20 dBm = 100 mW.
RSSICore
Received signal strength indicator: how strong a frame was at the receiver, in dBm.
Noise floor and SNRCore
Background radio noise sets a floor. Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is how far the signal sits above it, in dB.
Receiver sensitivityCore
The faintest signal the receiver can still decode. LoRa decodes below the noise floor (negative SNR), which is the root of its range.
Link budgetCore
Transmit power minus sensitivity: the most loss a link can absorb and still work.
Bandwidth and noiseDeeper
A wider channel collects more noise. A narrow 125 kHz channel helps sensitivity.

Worked example: ABOT's link budget

Transmit +20 dBm = 100 mW Decodable −137 dBm ≈ 0.00000000000002 mW Link budget = 20 − (−137) = 157 dB

Any path that loses less than 157 dB is, on average, heard. Card 4 turns this into a distance.

In ABOT (Table I)

  • 865 MHzIndia's de-licensed 865–867 MHz sub-band, chosen over 915 MHz.
  • +20 dBmTransmit power.
  • −137 dBmReceive sensitivity at SF12 / 125 kHz.

LoRa: range bought with airtime

LoRa is a long-range, low-power radio technique. It spreads each symbol over a long frequency sweep called a chirp, which lets the receiver recover very weak signals. The price is speed: a few hundred bits per second, and seconds of airtime per frame.

Concepts

Chirp spread spectrumCore
Each symbol is a tone that sweeps across the channel. Spreading makes the signal robust to noise and interference.
Spreading factor (SF7–SF12)Core
How long each chirp lasts. Each step up roughly doubles airtime and gains about 2.5 dB of sensitivity. SF12 is the slowest, longest-range setting.
BandwidthCore
The width of channel the chirp sweeps: 125 kHz in ABOT.
Data rateCore
At SF12 and 125 kHz, about 250 bits per second.
Time on airCore
How long one transmission occupies the channel.
MTUCore
Maximum payload per frame: 51 bytes at SF12 (data rate 0).
Duty cycleCore
The share of time a device may transmit. The limit the paper cites (ETSI EN 300 220-2) is 1%.
LoRa vs LoRaWANCore
LoRa is the radio. LoRaWAN is a network standard on top of it, routed through gateways. ABOT uses LoRa radios without LoRaWAN.
SF orthogonalityDeeper
Frames at different SFs interfere less with each other. ABOT puts every node on SF12, so they all compete for one channel.
Preamble and coding rateDeeper
A wake-up pattern before each frame and extra error-correction bits. They're why airtime exceeds the raw bit count.

Worked example: airtime and duty cycle

51 B × 8 = 408 bits at ≈ 250 bit/s ≈ 1.6 s of data + preamble and framing → 2.47 s per full frame Beacons: 4.4% of 30 s ≈ 1.3 s on air each At 1%: one 1.3 s beacon every ≈ 132 s

A compliant schedule means beacons roughly every 2.2 minutes instead of every 30 s, before any SOS traffic. Every result that depends on the beacon rate would shift.

In ABOT

  • SF12 / 125 kHzMaximum range setting (Table I).
  • 51 BMTU. Shapes the 42-byte gossip budget (card 6).
  • 2.47 sAirtime of a full SF12 frame (Simulation Backend).
  • ≈ 4.4% vs 1%Duty cycle used vs the limit. Not enforced in the simulation; a stated limitation.

Channel model: path loss and shadowing

A channel model predicts how much signal two nodes lose between them. ABOT uses the standard log-distance model plus random shadowing. Five damage presets make loss grow faster with distance and vary more from link to link.

Concepts

Path loss, PL(d)Core
Signal lost over a link of length d, in dB.
Log-distance modelCore
PL(d) = 31.2 + 10·nPL·log₁₀(d). 31.2 dB is the loss at 1 m at 865 MHz. Each tenfold increase in distance adds 10·nPL dB.
Path-loss exponent, nPLCore
How fast loss grows with distance. 2 is open space; cluttered or collapsed terrain is higher. Default 3.8.
Log-normal shadowing, σCore
A random dB offset for each link, drawn from a bell curve of spread σ. It stands in for walls, debris and other obstacles. Default 11 dB.
NLOSCore
Non-line-of-sight: no clear straight path between the nodes.
Severity presetsCore
(nPL, σ) pairs: intact (2.7, 7.0), moderate (3.0, 9.0), heavy (3.8, 11.0, the default), severe (4.2, 12.5), extreme (4.5, 14.0).
PDR vs distanceCore
Packet delivery ratio as a function of link length. This is Fig. 2.
Model vs validationDeeper
Parameters are grounded in published measurements (IEEE 802.15.4a, post-earthquake LoRa data) but not tested against ABOT hardware. Fig. 2 shows internal consistency only.
Shadowing vs fast fadingDeeper
Quick fluctuations from reflections (multipath) aren't modelled separately. Shadowing lumps slow, obstacle-driven variation into one random term.

Worked example: a 1500 m link, heavy damage

PL = 31.2 + 38 × log₁₀(1500) = 31.2 + 38 × 3.18 ≈ 151.9 dB Received = 20 − 151.9 ≈ −131.9 dBm Margin over −137 dBm ≈ 5.1 dB σ = 11 dB erases that margin ≈ 1/3 of the time → PDR ≈ 0.68

Fig. 2 reports 0.664. The 50% point is where the average received power equals sensitivity, so PL = 157 dB and d = 10(157 − 31.2) / (10·n).

Collisions and medium access

Every node shares one radio channel. When two transmissions overlap at a receiver they can destroy each other. The MAC (medium access control) layer decides when nodes may transmit and what happens when they clash.

Concepts

Shared medium, contentionCore
Many nodes, one channel: transmissions compete for it.
CollisionCore
Two or more frames overlap in time at the same receiver.
ALOHA and slotted ALOHACore
ALOHA: send whenever you have data; a collision loses the frame. Slotted ALOHA only starts transmissions at fixed slot boundaries, which halves the window for collisions.
Capture effectCore
If one colliding frame is much stronger than the rest, the receiver can still decode it.
Capture thresholdCore
ABOT's rule: the strongest frame (by mean RSSI) survives only if it beats the next by at least 6 dB, about 4× the power. Otherwise every colliding frame is lost.
Throughput ceilingDeeper
Classic slotted ALOHA uses at most about 37% (1/e) of the channel before collisions dominate.
Listen-before-talk (CSMA)Deeper
The other family of MAC schemes, where radios check the channel before sending. ABOT models ALOHA.
Hidden terminalDeeper
Two nodes that can't hear each other both send to a node between them and collide there.

Worked example: the 6 dB rule

−115 vs −119 dBm → 4 dB apart → both lost −115 vs −122 dBm → 7 dB apart → −115 frame survives

In ABOT

  • contention onEnabled in every reported result, in both simulators.
  • 6 dBCapture threshold, judged per receiver.
  • 2.47 sFrame length that the reference simulator overlaps in continuous time. The fast backend resolves collisions once per round (card 9).

Day 3 · about 1.5 h · Track A↑ map

Forwarding protocol

Forwarding is the rule a node follows when it hears a packet: pass it on or not, and to whom. ABOT mixes probabilistic broadcast (gossip) with directed hand-offs toward the destination, and adds several brakes so packets don't circulate forever.

Concepts

BeaconingCore
Each node periodically broadcasts a tiny "I'm here" packet, every 30 s. In ABOT a beacon carries only the sender's ID.
Header fieldsCore
Message ID, source (the previous hop, changes at every relay), destination, origin (the creator, never changes), type, priority, hop count, TTL, timestamp, RSSI, payload size. 34 bytes.
PriorityCore
SOS_ALERT carries the highest priority of the five message types.
Flooding and broadcast stormCore
If every node rebroadcasts everything, a dense network drowns in duplicates and collisions.
Probabilistic gossip, pCore
Each node rebroadcasts a new packet with probability p. ABOT: p = 0.75.
Counter-based suppression, KCore
A node waits a random moment before forwarding. If it overhears K = 3 copies first, it cancels its own.
TTL (time to live)Core
A hop limit, decremented at every relay. At zero the packet is dropped. ABOT starts at 16.
Duplicate detectionCore
A seen-cache of the last 256 message IDs per node; repeats are ignored.
Geo-greedy unicastCore
Hand the packet to the neighbour geographically closest to the destination.
Bimodal gossipDeeper
Haas et al. [9] showed gossip either reaches almost every node or dies out early, with a sharp switch near p ≈ 0.6–0.7 in moderately dense networks. 0.75 sits above the switch with margin for sparser, damaged meshes.
Greedy voidsDeeper
A known weakness of geographic forwarding in general: it stalls where no neighbour is closer to the destination.

Worked example: gossip vs flooding

30 neighbours hear a new SOS Flooding: 30 rebroadcasts Gossip p=0.75: ≈ 22.5 expected, 25% fewer (suppression at K = 3 trims more)

In ABOT

  • p 0.75 · K 3Gossip settings, chosen from Haas et al.'s threshold.
  • ≈ 25%Transmissions saved vs a full flood.
  • 34 B · TTL 16Header size and hop limit.
  • 42 BGossip payload: a 2-byte count + 10 entries × 4 bytes, inside the 51-byte MTU. Over 10 entries, the top 10 by magnitude are sent.
  • 256Seen-cache entries per node.

Link-state monitoring

To route well, a node needs to know which links are good right now. ABOT nodes measure this themselves from the beacons they hear, all the time, so routing can steer away from a weakening node before it fails outright.

Concepts

Link stateCore
The current quality of each link a node has.
PDR estimateCore
The share of a neighbour's packets that actually arrived.
Sliding windowCore
Only the most recent 50 packets count, so old history drops off. At one beacon per 30 s, that's about 25 minutes.
Per-neighbour RSSICore
How strong each neighbour's frames arrive.
Reachability fractionCore
The share of known neighbours heard recently.
Local derivationCore
Beacons carry only an ID, so the receiver computes all three metrics itself. Measuring costs no extra airtime.
Proactive vs reactiveCore
Reactive schemes recompute routes after something breaks. Proactive ones keep state current all the time. ABOT is continuous, which is one of the gaps the paper names.
Window trade-offDeeper
A long window gives a stable estimate but reacts slowly; a short one reacts fast but is noisy.
ETXDeeper
Expected transmission count, a classic mesh link metric equal to 1 ÷ delivery ratio. ABOT's routing cost (card 11) is the same idea.

Worked example: the window

50 beacons × 30 s = 1,500 s = 25 min Heard 33 of the last 50 → PDR ≈ 0.66 Neighbour dies → a simple count drops about 0.02 per missed beacon

That slow decay is why a "heard recently" reachability signal complements the PDR window.

In ABOT

  • 3 metricsPDR, RSSI, reachability, per node, from beacons alone.
  • 50 packetsPDR window, about 25 min at the SF12 beacon rate.
  • continuousUpdated every beacon, not only on detected failure.

Graphs and shortest paths

Route-finding algorithms see a network as a graph: points joined by lines, each line with a cost. The shortest path is the route with the smallest total cost, so whatever you choose as the cost decides what "shortest" means.

Concepts

Graph: vertices and edgesCore
Nodes are vertices; links are edges.
Edge weight (cost)Core
A number on each edge. Hop count gives every edge a cost of 1.
DegreeCore
How many neighbours a node has.
Dijkstra's algorithmCore
Grows outward from the start, always settling the cheapest unsettled node next, until it reaches the goal. Finds the cheapest path when costs aren't negative.
A* (A-star)Core
Dijkstra plus a heuristic: a guess of the remaining cost (here straight-line distance), so it explores toward the goal first.
Unit-disk graphDeeper
Connect any two nodes within radio range. ABOT's grid with a 1500 m range is one.
TiesDeeper
Several paths can share the lowest cost; which one wins depends on the order nodes are visited. This matters in card 11.
Admissible heuristicDeeper
A heuristic that never overestimates keeps A*'s answer optimal.

Worked example: where 30.7 and 44 come from

Range ÷ spacing = 1500 ÷ 400 = 3.75 grid steps Grid points within 3.75 steps of an interior node: 44 Edge and corner nodes have fewer → 10×10 mean 30.7

Both match the paper. A 10 × 10 grid at 400 m is 3.6 km across, so with a 1500 m reach most routes need only 2–4 hops and every planner succeeds.

In ABOT: the four baselines

  • dijkstra_staticDijkstra on hop count.
  • astar_staticA* on hop count, Euclidean heuristic.
  • dijkstra_dynamicDijkstra on PDR-weighted costs, with replanning (card 11).
  • astar_dynamicA* on PDR-weighted costs, with replanning.

Experimental Results, first paragraph; RL Environment (mean degree 30.7, maximum 44).

Day 4 · about 2 h · Track B↑ map

Simulation methods

Everything in the paper is simulated. The authors started with an event-by-event simulator (SimPy) that was too slow for reinforcement learning, then built a time-stepped backend that computes a whole round of radio outcomes at once. Both share the same protocol, channel, contention and failure models.

Concepts

Discrete-event simulationCore
A queue of timestamped events (frame starts and ends, timers), processed one at a time in continuous time. ABOT's reference backend uses SimPy.
Time-stepped simulationCore
The clock advances in fixed steps and everything due is processed together. One RL step is 10 s.
VectorisationCore
Computing a whole batch of link outcomes in one array operation instead of a loop. This is where the speed comes from.
Monte Carlo evaluationCore
Run many randomised episodes and study the spread of results.
Random seedCore
Fixes the random draws so different methods face exactly the same scenario.
Failure modelsCore
Sudden: a Poisson process, random and memoryless, at λ = 1/3600 s⁻¹ (about one failure per node per hour). Graceful: the battery drains linearly over 300 s, and below 20% charge delivery probability scales down by b/0.20. Mixed (50/50) is the default. Burst: everything within one grid cell of an epicentre.
SpeedupCore
Wall-clock time of the reference divided by the fast backend's, for the same workload.
Backend equivalenceCore
Do both simulators produce the same distribution of results? Tested with the KS test (card 10).
Fidelity vs speedDeeper
Coarser timing buys speed at the cost of exact collision behaviour.
Sim-to-real gapDeeper
Anything not modelled, such as hardware quirks, real terrain or duty-cycle enforcement, can change results on real devices.

In ABOT

  • 7.3×Speedup with contention on, the setting behind every result.
  • 303×Speedup with contention off.
  • λ sweepFailure rates {0, 1/3600, 1/1800} s⁻¹ move neither figure.
  • p = 0.98KS test, alive-node count: indistinguishable.
  • p < 10⁻³KS test, PDR and delivery count: different.
  • 100 → 900Nodes: 10 × 10 for the main results, up to 30 × 30 for scalability.
ShowsThe fast backend is quick enough for RL and matches the reference on node survival, which is what the agent trains against.
Doesn't showSpeed against other simulators (the comparison is with the authors' own reference), or matching absolute traffic under contention.

Day 5 · about 1.5 h · Track B↑ map

Evaluation statistics

Results are distributions over many random episodes, so the paper uses statistics to judge whether a difference is real and how big it is. It uses rank-based (non-parametric) tools because step counts are small whole numbers with no bell curve.

Concepts

Median and IQRCore
The middle value, and the range of the middle half. "3 [2, 3]" means median 3 steps, middle half between 2 and 3. Robust to a few extreme runs.
Ceiling effectCore
Every agent reaches a safe zone about 100% of the time, so success can't separate them. They're compared on steps.
p-valueCore
How likely a difference this big would be by chance if the methods were really equal. Below 0.05 is the usual bar.
Significance vs sizeCore
Significant means unlikely to be chance, not large or important.
Mann–Whitney UCore
Compares two groups by ranking all values together. No bell-curve assumption.
Rank-biserial rrbCore
Effect size for Mann–Whitney, from −1 to +1. In Table II, negative favours PPO.
Multiple comparisons, HolmCore
More tests mean more lucky hits. Holm–Bonferroni raises the bar step by step; pcorr is the corrected value.
Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS)Core
Compares two whole distributions by the largest gap between their cumulative curves. Used for backend equivalence.
Confidence intervalCore
A range that very likely contains the true value, usually 95%.
Pearson rCore
How closely two quantities move together. Reward vs path length: −0.987 to −0.993, so steps is the primary metric.
BootstrapDeeper
Resample the data with replacement thousands of times to see how much an estimate wobbles.
Matched designDeeper
Giving both arms the same seeds removes scenario luck from the comparison.

Worked example: Holm vs Bonferroni

Three tests, sorted: p = 0.010, 0.020, 0.040 Holm bars: 0.05/3, 0.05/2, 0.05/1 = 0.0167, 0.025, 0.05 → all three pass Bonferroni: 0.0167 for each → only the first passes

Table II, row by row

  • −0.358 · 2.5×10⁻⁵10 × 10: PPO takes fewer steps than dijkstra_dynamic. Medium effect, essentially not chance.
  • +0.184 · 0.04710 × 10: PPO takes slightly more steps than the three fastest planners. Small effect, just under the bar.
  • −0.281 · 1.9×10⁻³15 × 15: PPO again beats dijkstra_dynamic.
  • +0.137 · 0.25515 × 15: no detectable difference from the three fastest planners.

Day 5 · about 2 h★ Main result↑ map

Link-aware routing: the ablation

The paper's central technical claim: putting live link quality into the route-finder's edge costs is what improves delivery. It is tested by ablation: the same Dijkstra planner, same seeds, same simulator, with and without live PDR in its costs.

Concepts

Static costCore
Hop count. Every link looks equal, however weak.
PDR-weighted costCore
Weaker links cost more. The implementation uses 1 ÷ PDR, so a PDR 0.5 link costs twice a perfect one.
Replanning triggerCore
The dynamic planners recompute if a planned hop dies, or its PDR falls below 0.5, within the next 3 hops.
Empty first snapshotCore
Both dynamic planners plan their first route before any link data exists, so every edge looks equal. Any gain has to come from replanning.
AblationCore
Remove one ingredient, keep everything else fixed, measure the change. Here the ingredient is live PDR in the cost.
Mean link PDRCore
The outcome measure: average PDR of the links a route actually used.
Matched subsetCore
Keep only the 43 seeds where both arms planned the identical first route, so replanning is the only difference.
Path-length confoundDeeper
The dynamic planner takes longer routes and PDR falls with path length, so the raw ratio understates the gain. Matching on steps widens it to 1.9–2.6×.
Fidelity checkDeeper
The paper's "identical on all 100" means the static planners took exactly the same steps re-run on the fast backend as on the reference. It does not mean static and dynamic take equal steps (2.17 vs 3.23).
Tie-breakingDeeper
Equal-cost paths are resolved by the iteration order of an unordered set, which depends on Python's hash seed. Over 8 seeds, dijkstra_dynamic's PDR ranges 0.264–0.294.
Why the A* pair is out of scopeDeeper
Its trigger fired in 3 of 100 episodes; both arms planned the same first route in all 100 and the same realised path in 97. A ratio of 1.03× (p = 0.886) is evidence neither way.

In ABOT

  • 0.267 vs 0.145Mean link PDR, dynamic vs static Dijkstra. ≈1.8–1.9× at intact through heavy, ≈1.5× at severe and extreme.
  • 1.50×The 43-seed subset: 0.3098 vs 0.2067, p = 0.0057, rrb = −0.346. The cleanest number.
  • 3.23 vs 2.17Mean steps, dynamic vs the fastest planners. The price of better links.
  • 0.28 → 0.218dijkstra_dynamic's PDR is flat from intact to heavy, then drops 23% in one step at severe (0.216 at extreme). Other agents move less than 0.02.
ShowsIn simulation, live link state is where the delivery gain comes from.
Doesn't showThe gain on hardware, or that learning (PPO) or gossip adds to it.

Reinforcement learning fundamentals

Reinforcement learning (RL) trains an agent by trial and error. At each step the agent observes its environment, picks an action and receives a reward. Over many episodes it learns a policy that maximises total reward. In ABOT the agent decides which neighbour to hand a message to next.

Concepts

Agent and environmentCore
ABOT's environment wraps the mesh simulator behind the standard Gymnasium interface: reset() starts an episode, step(action) advances it.
State vs observationCore
The state is everything about the world; the observation is what the agent actually sees. ABOT's agent sees 24 local numbers.
ActionCore
What the agent may do each step: pick one of 8 neighbour slots.
Reward and returnCore
Reward is the per-step score; return is the total over an episode.
EpisodeCore
One attempt, from reset until a safe zone is reached or 200 steps pass.
Discount factor γCore
How much future rewards count. With γ = 0.99, a reward 100 steps away still counts about 37% (0.99¹⁰⁰ ≈ 0.37).
Policy πCore
The learned rule mapping observations to actions.
Value V(s) and Q(s, a)Core
V is the expected return from a state. Q is the expected return from taking action a in state s.
Exploration vs exploitationCore
Try new actions to learn, or use what already works.
Reward shapingCore
Intermediate rewards that guide learning toward the real goal.
MDP and POMDPDeeper
The formal model: states, actions, transitions, rewards. Partially observable when the agent sees only part of the state, as here.
Deterministic evaluationDeeper
Training samples actions with randomness; evaluation can always take the single most likely action. This matters for the livelock (card 14).
Paper vs implementationDeeper
The paper says "+5 per new SOS delivered". The implementation gives +5 at most once per step if any new SOS was delivered.

Worked example: why reward tracks steps

Hypothetical: 3 steps, link PDR 0.3, SOS delivered +100 − 3 × 1 − 0.5 × 3 × (1 − 0.3) + 5 = 100 − 3 − 1.05 + 5 = 100.95

The +100 dominates and each extra step costs about 1–1.5 points, so reward is almost a function of path length (Pearson r −0.987 to −0.993). That's why steps-to-safe-zone is the primary metric.

In ABOT: the reward

  • +100Reaching a safe zone (terminal bonus).
  • −1Each step.
  • −20Zero reachable neighbours, or choosing a dead or missing neighbour.
  • +5New SOS delivered.
  • −0.5·(1 − PDR)Each step, at the current node's PDR. A soft preference, scaled below the step cost.
  • 10 s · 200Seconds per step; step cap (2,000 s ≈ 33 min). SOS every 10 steps, gossip every 5, beacons every 3. Two safe zones.

PPO and how it was trained

PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) is a widely used RL algorithm. It trains two neural networks side by side: an actor that picks actions and a critic that estimates how good situations are. It caps how far each update can move the policy, which keeps training stable.

Concepts

MLP policyCore
A small neural network: 2 hidden layers of 64 tanh units.
Policy gradientCore
Raise the probability of actions that did better than expected; lower the rest.
Actor-criticCore
The actor is the policy. The critic estimates V(s) so the actor knows what "better than expected" means.
AdvantageCore
How much better an action turned out than the critic predicted.
Clipping (0.2)Core
Limits each update to roughly a ±20% change in action probabilities. This is the "proximal" in PPO.
Entropy bonus (0.01)Core
A small reward for staying random, which keeps the agent exploring.
Rollout, minibatch, epochsCore
Collect experience, then make several learning passes over it in small chunks before collecting more.
Parallel environmentsCore
7 copies of the environment in one process, each contributing 200 steps per rollout.
Explained varianceCore
How much of the variation in real returns the critic predicts. 1 is perfect, 0 is no better than predicting the average, negative is worse than that.
GAE λ (0.95)Deeper
Generalised advantage estimation blends short- and long-horizon advantage estimates, trading bias against noise.
Learning rate (3 × 10⁻⁴)Deeper
Step size for each network update.
Default hyperparametersDeeper
All are Stable-Baselines3 defaults, not tuned. Tuning is untested.
On-policyDeeper
PPO learns only from fresh data gathered by its current policy, so it needs a lot of simulation. Hence the fast backend.
Which model was evaluatedDeeper
The baseline comparison and severity sweep used the seed-0 model, according to the result files; the paper doesn't say. All 3 seeds appear in training and the gossip ablation.

Worked example: training arithmetic

7 envs × 200 steps = 1,400 transitions per update 200,000 ÷ 1,400 ≈ 143 updates per seed 1,400 ÷ 64 ≈ 22 minibatches × 10 epochs ≈ 220 gradient steps per update

In ABOT

  • 3 × 200kThree seeds, 200,000 steps each summed across the 7 workers, on CPU.
  • γ 0.99 · λ 0.95Discount and GAE settings.
  • clip 0.2 · ent 0.01Update cap and exploration bonus.
  • −0.67, 0.18, 0.71Final explained variance per seed. One critic is worse than guessing the average, one is weak, one is decent: not converged for at least one seed. A stated limitation.

ABOT's RL design and its failure modes

How ABOT turns routing into an RL problem, and where that design holds the agent back: what it sees, what it can choose, and two asymmetries that both favour the classical planners.

The 24-number observation

  • 9Base: normalised x and y; local PDR; reachable-neighbour count; battery; distance to the nearest safe zone; cosine and sine of the bearing to it; elapsed fraction of the episode.
  • 8Neighbour PDRs, sorted by node ID.
  • 7Extras: unit vector to the nearest alive gateway (x, y); hop count to it; local node density; PDR trend; minimum neighbour PDR; maximum neighbour RSSI.

Concepts

Discrete(8) action spaceCore
Each action indexes the ID-sorted neighbour list.
Action-space truncationCore
8 slots against a mean of 30.7 neighbours (maximum 44). Most neighbours are unreachable as choices.
Invalid actionCore
Choosing a dead or missing neighbour: −20, and the agent stays where it is.
Two asymmetriesCore
The planners get a global network snapshot; PPO gets only its local observation. The planners may pick any neighbour; PPO picks from 8 slots. Both favour the planners, so PPO's result is a lower bound.
LivelockCore
A loop where the agent keeps acting but makes no progress. See the cycle on the right.
Action maskingCore
Remove invalid actions from the choice set before the policy picks. The standard fix, along with a variable-size neighbour set.
Addressable but unobservableDeeper
Indices must survive node failures, so dead neighbours stay in the action list. In the implementation, the neighbour-PDR slots are filled from neighbours actually heard, so after failures slot i and action i can be different nodes.
Q-value gossipDeeper
Carries the critic's V(s) per node (the code calls it a "Q-table"). Encoded as a 16-bit node index plus a 16-bit quantised value, top 10 by magnitude, 42 bytes, flooded with TTL 4.
Merge rule (not wired)Deeper
Qnew = (1 − α)·Qlocal + α·Qreceived, α = 0.3: a moving average. Implemented but not connected to the receive path, so the ablation tests transport cost only.
GossipCallbackDeeper
During training, averages value estimates across the 7 workers between PPO updates.

The livelock, as a loop

  1. The agent observes its situation.
  2. The deterministic policy picks action 0.
  3. Action 0's neighbour is dead: rejected, −20, the agent doesn't move.
  4. The next observation is almost identical.
  5. Back to step 1. The isolation penalty doesn't fire (other neighbours are alive) and no gateway is reached, so nothing breaks the loop.

It shows up in one seed block at every severity level, so it comes from the action-space design, not from damage.

In ABOT

  • 3 [2, 3]PPO median steps at 10 × 10, vs 2 [2, 3] for the three fastest planners and 3 [2, 4] for dijkstra_dynamic.
  • 4 [3, 4.25]PPO at 15 × 15, vs 3 [2, 4] and 4 [3, 6].
  • 0.996 vs 1.000PPO success at every severity preset vs the classical planners.
  • ≈ 16.9 / 22.9Seed-3 block mean reward (intact–heavy / severe–extreme), against 101.4–102.2 for the other four blocks. Sweep means 84.6 / 86.0; median 101.5.
  • 2.487 vs 2.497Steps, gossip on vs off (p = 0.842). Reward 101.42 vs 101.27 (p = 0.826). No difference.
ShowsThe simulator can train an agent that solves the task, with a narrow claim: PPO beats dijkstra_dynamic at both scales.
Doesn't showThat RL beats classical routing, or that gossip helps.
