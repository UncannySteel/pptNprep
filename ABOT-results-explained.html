The project in one minute

The problem

After a big disaster, phone towers and the internet often go down just when survivors and rescuers most need to talk.

The idea

Cheap LoRa radios that pass short messages from one to the next, like a bucket brigade. There is no central tower (no gateway): every radio sends its own messages and relays other people's.

The twist

Each radio keeps a running link-quality score for its neighbours, and the route-finder uses those live scores all the time, not only after something breaks.

The AI part

The authors also trained an AI route-picker (PPO, a trial-and-error learning method). The paper calls this part exploratory.

How it was tested

Entirely in a computer simulation. No real radios were used. The authors built a fast simulator so the AI could be trained.

The task in each test

Start at a random radio and hop, radio by radio, to one of two safe zones (rescue points) in the corners of the grid. Count the hops and check that it arrives.

  • 100radios on a 10×10 grid (main tests); 225 on 15×15
  • 400 mbetween neighbouring radios on the grid
  • 1.5 kmradio range, so each radio has about 31 neighbours (up to 44)
  • 1–3hops for a typical trip by the fastest route-finders
  • 5damage levels, from intact to extreme
  • 100trips per route-finder per grid in the main comparison

The "1–3 hops" figure comes from the project's result files (commaware_main_10x10.csv); the paper gives means and medians instead. Everything else in this block is in the paper.

The six results

Ordered from the strongest to the weakest claim. Each card says what was tested, what happened, what it means, what not to say, and then (in amber) what the project files add.

1

Using live link scores changes which routes get picked

The paper's main supported result. Paper sections V-C and VII.

What was tested

The same route-finder (Dijkstra, the classic shortest-route method a sat-nav uses) was run in two versions on the same 100 test trips:

  • Static: counts hops only and takes the shortest route.
  • Dynamic: also weighs each link by its live quality score, and re-plans if a radio on the next three hops dies or its score drops below 0.5.

What happened

0.267 vs 0.145average link score, dynamic vs static
≈1.8×higher with live scores
1.50×fairest test (43 trips), p = 0.0057
  • Across damage levels, the gap is about 1.8–1.9× from intact to heavy and about 1.5× at severe and extreme.
  • Fairest test: in 43 of the 100 trips, both versions planned the same first route, so the only difference left is re-planning. There the scores were 0.3098 vs 0.2067, i.e. 1.50×. A p-value of 0.0057 means a gap this big would almost never appear by chance.
  • When trips of the same length are compared, the gap widens to 1.9–2.6×.
  • The A* pair (another classic route-finder, tested the same way) shows nothing (1.03×), because its re-planning rule fired in only 3 of 100 trips. The paper says this pair can't be used as evidence.

What it means

A route-finder that listens to live link quality ends up on noticeably better-quality links than one that only counts hops. This is the one result the paper stands on.

Be careful

Don't say "it delivers 1.8× more messages." The number is a link-quality score, not a count of messages that arrived. Say "it chooses routes over links with about 1.8 times higher quality scores." The dynamic version also takes longer routes: 3.23 hops on average against 2.17.

  • Helps
    Longer trips score lower for every route-finder (Dijkstra static: 0.253, 0.139 and 0.090 for 1, 2 and 3 hops). So the dynamic version wins even though its longer routes work against it. Matched by length: 0.362 vs at most 0.161 for 2-hop trips; 0.233 vs at most 0.120 for 3-hop trips. FINDINGS.md §5; commaware_main_10x10.csv

    The test is partly circular. The dynamic version picks routes using this score, and is then graded on the same score. It's a bit like a student who chooses which questions to answer and is marked only on those. baselines.py:339-342; runner.py:103-104

    The score is a radio's average over all its ~30 links, not the quality of the link actually used. A link that has never been tried counts as perfect (1.0). timestep.py:720-733

    The dynamic version never found a shorter route than the static one, and took a longer one in 63 of 100 trips. In 10 of the 23 trips where the safe zone was one hop away, it took 2 or more hops. Don't claim the extra hops are "worth it". commaware_main_10x10.csv; baselines.csv
2

The AI route-picker is in the same range as the classic methods, not better than the fastest

Paper section V-A and Table II.

What was tested

PPO against four classic route-finders (Dijkstra and A*, each static and dynamic), 100 trips each, on the 10×10 grid and the larger 15×15 grid, at the default "heavy" damage level.

What happened

  • Everyone arrives. All five reach a safe zone in essentially every trip, so they are compared on how many hops they take.
  • 10×10, average hops: three fastest planners 2.17, PPO 2.48, Dijkstra dynamic 3.23.
  • 15×15, average hops: three fastest planners 3.23–3.25, PPO 3.56, Dijkstra dynamic 4.40.
  • Statistics (Table II): PPO beats Dijkstra dynamic at both sizes. It is slightly worse than the three fastest at 10×10 (adjusted p = 0.047, just under the usual 0.05 cut-off), and shows no measurable difference from them at 15×15 (p = 0.255).

What it means

The AI learned a sensible way to route, but simple shortest-path methods are as good or better. It ranks 4th of 5 at both grid sizes. The paper is upfront about this and calls the AI part exploratory.

Be careful

Don't say "PPO matches the classical planners." At 10×10 it is measurably behind the fastest ones. Say "in the same range as the classic methods, slightly behind the fastest at 10×10, no significant difference at 15×15." The paper calls PPO's result a "lower bound" (handicapped, so the true value could be better). See the amber block before leaning on that.

Project files only · not in the paper
  • Weakness
    The "lower bound" argument is weaker than it sounds. The paper says PPO is handicapped twice: it sees only local information, and it can only choose among 8 neighbours. But (1) PPO is given a hop count to the nearest safe zone that is worked out from the whole network map, and (2) a "policy" that always picks the first of its 8 choices matches PPO's medians (3 hops at 10×10, 4 at 15×15), though its averages are a little worse (2.71 and 4.07 against 2.48 and 3.56). That works because the 8 choices are the lowest-numbered neighbours and both safe zones sit in the lowest-numbered row. So the 8-choice limit doesn't stop PPO finding a good next hop. state.py:164-166; redteam/seed_starts.py
  • Know it
    The planner that uses choices beyond the first 8 most often (Dijkstra dynamic, 23.6% of moves) is the one PPO beats. The fast A* dynamic goes beyond 8 in under 1% of moves. results_rev2/action_space_audit.json
  • Know it
    Every PPO result in the comparison and the damage sweep comes from one trained model (training run "seed 0"). That is the run whose value-estimator (the "critic") converged worst: −0.67 in the paper's limitations list. results_rev1/convergence_summary.json
  • Know it
    The task is easy to learn. PPO's average reward was already 81–89 after its first 1,400 training steps and ended near 101 after 200,000. results_rev1/tb/seed_*/progress.csv
  • Helps
    At 15×15 the two static planners each had one bad trip: 8–9 hops and a reward of about 72–73, most likely from repeatedly trying a radio that had died. A* dynamic took 5 hops on the same trip, and no re-planning planner or AI trip ever scored below 92.3. It is one trip, so it's a worst-case example, not a statistical result. results_15x15/FINDINGS.md §5
3

Making the damage worse barely changes the outcome

Paper section V-B, the severity sweep.

What was tested

Five damage levels (intact, moderate, heavy, severe, extreme). More damage means radio signals fade faster with distance and vary more from place to place, as rubble would cause. 250 trips per route-finder per level.

What happened

  • Success stays at about 100% at every level.
  • Only Dijkstra dynamic's link score moves: about 0.28 from intact to heavy, then 0.218 at severe and 0.216 at extreme. That is a single 23% drop at one step, not a gradual slide. Everyone else moves by less than 0.02.
  • PPO succeeds in 99.6% of trips at every level. Four of its five groups of trials average 101–102 reward. The fourth group averages about 17 (intact to heavy) and 23 (severe and extreme).
  • Why that group is low: a livelock, a stuck loop. The AI keeps choosing a neighbour that has died, gets a penalty, sees almost the same situation, and chooses it again.

What it means

In this setup, damage weakens the long links but doesn't stop trips. The AI's weak spot comes from its fixed list of 8 choices, not from damage: the stuck loop happens at every damage level.

Be careful

The abstract mentions "limitations of RL-based routing under extreme network collapse". The results don't show a weakness specific to severe damage. Say "the AI's weak point is a stuck loop caused by its fixed 8-choice list, and it happens at every damage level."

Project files only · not in the paper
  • Weakness
    Success is 100% by design. A hop succeeds whenever the next radio is alive. Link quality never makes a hop fail, so damage can't stop a trip. env.py:311-313
  • Helps
    Why the drop happens exactly at "severe". From the paper's own Fig. 2, the distance where a link works half the time is 2.0 km at heavy (beyond the 1.5 km neighbour range) but 1.03 km at severe (inside it). So at severe, the longest links in range start failing. This explains the step in plain terms.
  • Know it
    The simulator used for these runs has no random fading. At intact through heavy, every link within range works at least 99.96% of the time; at severe, links longer than about 1.1 km fail; at extreme, longer than about 0.8 km. timestep.py:157-169; context.md §15
  • Know it
    The static planners take exactly the same hops at all five levels in 250 of 250 trips. Radios rarely die during a trip: the chance that one on the route dies is about 1–3%, because trips last only tens of simulated seconds. commaware_severity_episodes.csv
  • Know it
    The low PPO group is one stuck trip, not a whole bad group. In that group, 49 of 50 trips reach the safe zone as usual; the one that gets stuck collects penalties until the time limit and drags the group's average down. That's why the median stays at 101.5. results_rev2/seed3_outlier.json
4

The new simulator is fast enough to train the AI

Paper section IV.

What was tested

The original simulator (SimPy) handles every radio transmission as a separate event, which is accurate but slow. The new one (the timestep backend) processes all radios together, one time step at a time. The authors timed both and checked whether they give the same answers.

What happened

up to 7.3×faster with collisions modelled (used for every result)
up to 303×faster without collisions
  • Changing how often radios fail doesn't change the speed-up.
  • Agreement check (a KS test over 20 runs): the two simulators agree on how many radios stay alive (p = 0.98), but disagree on message-delivery numbers (p < 0.001).

What it means

The fast simulator makes AI training practical. It matches the original on which radios are alive, which is what the AI learns from. Absolute delivery numbers from either simulator shouldn't be trusted.

Be careful

The speed-up is against the authors' own reference simulator, not against standard network simulators such as ns-3 or OMNeT++. Say "up to 7.3 times faster than our reference simulator."

Project files only · not in the paper
  • Weakness
    7.3× is the smallest grid (25 radios). The speed-up shrinks as the grid grows: 4.7× at 10×10, the size used for the results; 4.0× at 15×15; 3.5–4.5× from 20×20 to 30×30. results_rev1/scalability.csv
  • Know it
    Why 303× is so large: the reference simulator gets much slower when collisions are switched off (about 14.3 s per run at 15×15, against about 0.5 s with collisions on), most likely because far more messages survive and have to be processed. The 303× was measured at 15×15. scalability_nocontention.csv
  • Know it
    With collisions on, the reference simulator ran all the way to 30×30 (one of the 15 runs at that size timed out). Only the no-collision runs timed out from 20×20 up. scalability.csv; scalability_nocontention.csv
  • Weakness
    The fast simulator sends every radio's "I'm here" beacon at the same instant, so they collide: in a check, none of 3,068 beacon receptions got through. It also has no random fading. Both make it simpler than the real world. timestep.py:452-463, 157-169; redteam/beacon_round.py

Sharing learned values between radios ("gossip") makes no difference

Paper section IV-A.

What was tested

Radios can broadcast the AI's estimate of "how good is this spot" to their neighbours. The AI was trained and tested with this sharing switched on and off: 3 training runs and 300 trips per arm.

What happened

MeasureGossip onGossip offp
Reached safe zone100%100%1.0
Average hops2.4872.4970.842
Average reward101.42101.270.826

What it means

The system works fine without gossip. This doesn't show gossip helps. The paper itself says the step where a radio would use the values it receives isn't connected yet.

Be careful

Don't say gossip helps, or that it was shown to be cheap. Say "Gossip made no difference; its merge step isn't connected yet."

Project files only · not in the paper
  • Weakness
    During the 300 test trips with gossip "on", no gossip message was actually sent, because each trip starts with an empty value table. A few go out during training, but nothing ever reads them. So avoid repeating the paper's line that the "on" arm "pays gossip's airtime". gossip.py:200-222; env.py:280-288

The radio model behaves sensibly

Paper section V-D and Fig. 2.

What was tested

How often a single radio link gets a message through, plotted against distance, for each damage level.

What happened

Damage levelLink works half the time atSuccess at 1.5 km
Intact45.5 km100%
Moderate15.5 km99.9%
Heavy (default)2.0 km66.4%
Severe1.03 km28.6%
Extreme637 m10.9%

At the 400 m grid spacing, links work more than 73% of the time at every level.

What it means

More damage means shorter reach, and longer distance means more failures, as the textbook model predicts. This checks the model is internally consistent. It doesn't prove it matches real radios.

Be careful

Don't call this "validated" against reality. The paper's own words are "internal consistency of the channel model only".

Project files only · not in the paper
  • Know it
    The curve is calculated straight from the radio formulas, not from running the mesh simulator. The figure caption's "timestep backend" is misleading; drop it from your slide caption. scripts/make_fig4_pdr_distance.py:15

Project files only · the biggest thing to understand

What was never measured: whether an SOS reaches a rescuer

The abstract says the results "demonstrate improved packet delivery performance", and the conclusion speaks of a "measured delivery gain". No experiment counts whether a distress message actually arrives at a rescue point, or how long it takes.

  • The routes don't carry messages. A "trip" moves the agent's position from radio to radio. Nothing is transmitted along the route that each method picks.
  • SOS messages are simulated, but separately. They are broadcast to everyone, with no destination, whichever route-finder is in charge. Nothing checks whether one reached a safe zone.
  • The result files hold only four things per trip: arrived or not, hops, reward, and the link score.

So the honest claim is "live link scores change which routes get chosen", not "more messages get through". The natural next step, before hardware, is to send each SOS along each method's route over the radio model, let hops fail, and count arrivals and delay.

Limits the paper admits

From paper section VI. These are safe to say on stage because the paper already says them.

LimitIn plain words
Simulation onlyNo real radios were used. Results cover up to 900 simulated radios.
Airtime ruleBeacons every 30 s at SF12 take about 4.4% of airtime, above the 1% limit in the European rule ETSI EN 300 220-2. A compliant schedule would change every beacon-based number.
8-choice limitThe AI picks from 8 neighbours, but radios have about 31. This fixed list causes the stuck loop.
AI training not settledThe AI's value-estimator didn't converge: scores of −0.67, 0.18 and 0.71 across the three training runs (1.0 would be perfect).
AdoptionPeople have to carry and use the devices for a mesh to exist.

Words to know

The only jargon you need for the results part, in plain words.

LoRa
A low-power radio that sends small messages several kilometres, slowly. Think text messages, not voice.
Mesh
A network where devices pass messages to each other instead of through a central tower.
Gateway
The central receiver in normal LoRa networks (LoRaWAN). ABOT doesn't need one.
Hop
One radio-to-radio jump. "Steps to safe zone" in the paper means hops.
Safe zone
A rescue point the trip has to reach. There are two, in grid corners.
PDR (packet delivery ratio)
Strictly, the share of messages that arrive. In the routing results it's used for a link-quality score, so call it that.
Dijkstra, A*
Classic shortest-route methods, the kind a sat-nav uses. A* adds a "head roughly towards the goal" hint.
Static / dynamic
Static plans once by counting hops. Dynamic weighs links by live quality and re-plans when the route ahead degrades.
PPO
A reinforcement-learning method: the AI tries actions, gets points (reward) for good outcomes, and slowly learns what works.
Reward
The AI's score per trip: +100 for arriving, −1 per hop, penalties for bad choices. It mostly mirrors the hop count.
Livelock
A stuck loop: the AI keeps repeating the same useless move.
Seed
The starting value for the simulation's randomness. Different seeds give different scenarios; a "seed block" is a group of trials run from one seed.
Median, IQR
The middle value, and the range covering the middle half of results. Used because a few extreme trips can distort an average.
p-value
How likely a gap this big would be if there were really no difference. Below 0.05 is the usual bar for "real".
Adjusted p
A p-value corrected because several comparisons were tested at once (here, Holm correction).
KS test
A check of whether two sets of results look like they come from the same source. Used to compare the two simulators.
Path-loss exponent, shadowing
How fast a signal weakens with distance (higher means faster), and how much it varies randomly from place to place.
SF12
LoRa's slowest, longest-range setting. Each message takes about 2.5 seconds to send.
Duty cycle
The share of time a radio is allowed to transmit. Europe's rule for this band is 1%.
Collisions (contention)
Two radios sending at once garble each other. The stronger one survives only if it's clearly louder (6 dB).
Gossip
Radios passing information to their neighbours, who pass it on, like a rumour spreading.
Critic, explained variance
The part of the AI that estimates "how good is this situation", and a score of how well it does that (1.0 is perfect; below 0 is worse than guessing the average).
